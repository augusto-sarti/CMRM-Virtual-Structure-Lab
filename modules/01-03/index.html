Module 1 · From sound to features

The waveform contains thousands of samples per second. Short-time descriptors condense this detail into a manageable time series. Choose a descriptor and listen while its trajectory is displayed. Feature plots are measured from the selected recording, not prearranged diagrams.

Load a recording to see its descriptors.

Mathematical note

RMS: √(Σx[n]²/N). Centroid: Σfₖ|Xₖ| / Σ|Xₖ|. Spectral flux: Σ max(0, |Xₖ(t)| − |Xₖ(t−1)|), here displayed after compression. Chroma groups spectral energy by pitch class and is sensitive to harmonics. These are acoustic estimators, not direct descriptions of musical motives or formal functions.

Module 2 · What does it mean for two passages to be similar?

Select two regions of the recording and compare their average descriptor values. Then listen to each region. These are deliberately simple summaries: the differences between the listening judgment and the numerical result are part of the exercise.

Load a recording to compare two passages.

A note about limitations: averages discard the order of notes and chords. Matching average chroma does not prove that two excerpts contain the same musical idea. For ordered relationships, use the matrix below; temporal alignment is introduced in Module 6.

Module 3 · Discover recurrence with a self-similarity matrix

Rows and columns are successive audio windows. Bright off-diagonal patterns suggest related passages; a bright diagonal always appears because each window matches itself.

Choose a recording to build the matrix.

Three questions for discussion

1. Can a rise in energy indicate a formal boundary, or merely an accent? 2. When two passages sound related but their average chroma differs, could transposition be involved? 3. What recurring patterns can you locate away from the main diagonal of the self-similarity matrix?

Module 4 extends the same matrix with novelty detection and listener-supplied boundary annotations.

Optional extension: novelty curve

The same audio engine computes an introductory novelty curve from a local checkerboard comparison. It is available in the dedicated Module 4 laboratory, where it can be contrasted against listener judgments.

From descriptions to interpretations

None of the features or similarities above automatically defines a section, motive, or formal function. The reader's task is to compare measured acoustic evidence with musical listening. Modules 4–7 proceed from recurrence to novelty, clustering, temporal alignment, and interpreted form.

Suggested source recording: V. Gao, Für Elise, Wikimedia Commons, CC0 1.0. Feature calculations are performed locally by your browser. This module does not package the original recording; on first use an internet connection or downloaded audio file is necessary.
