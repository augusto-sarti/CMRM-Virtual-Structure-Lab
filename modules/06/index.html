4 · Guided listening and interpretation

First compare linear matching with DTW. Does the DTW path bend away from the diagonal when the timing changes? Then try RMS-only versus chroma: does matching the pattern of loudness necessarily recover the same musical events? Finally, upload a second performance of the same passage and ask whether expressive rubato changes the alignment path.

Important distinction: DTW accounts for local differences in timing, but it does not automatically solve transposition, changes in instrumentation, unreliable features, or changes in musical form. A lower DTW cost does not prove that two performances share the same interpretation.
Optional mathematics: what does DTW minimize?

Let xi and yj be successive feature vectors. Define a local distance d(i,j). The accumulated cost satisfies D(i,j) = d(i,j) + min(D(i−1,j), D(i,j−1), D(i−1,j−1)), with suitable boundary conditions. Backtracking from the final cell gives a monotone alignment path. The normalized path cost displayed here divides total cost by the number of visited cells; it should be used as a comparison aid, not as an absolute measure of musical identity.

The diagonal baseline compares equally spaced relative positions. The controlled demonstration builds B by sampling the same extracted feature sequence along a nonlinear time map. It is therefore an idealized test of timing invariance, not an independent musical performance.

Chapter 7 connection: a musical idea may remain recognizable despite expressive timing differences. A representation and its matching rule together determine what counts as the “same” musical event.
