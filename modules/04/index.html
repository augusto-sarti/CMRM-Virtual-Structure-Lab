Listen and mark at least two perceived boundaries. Does the algorithm identify them using chroma? Do the results change when using brightness or energy? Does a clear musical return necessarily produce a strong novelty peak?

Core lesson. Structure is not contained in the sound as a list of ready-made labels. We construct representations of acoustic evidence and then interpret what their relationships might mean musically.
Optional mathematical and technical explanation

The waveform is divided into non-overlapping 0.75-second comparison intervals. Within each interval, three short-window FFT spectra are extracted and summarized. RMS estimates signal energy; spectral centroid estimates the mean frequency weighted by spectral magnitude; chroma groups spectral energy into twelve pitch classes; positive spectral flux estimates sudden changes in magnitude spectra. Chroma here is an approximate, educational audio estimator; strong harmonics and piano resonance can contribute to neighboring or harmonic pitch classes.

For feature vectors fi, each matrix element is a similarity S(i,j). Chroma uses cosine similarity, while scalar features use exponentially decreasing similarity with a scale determined by the feature’s distribution. For novelty at time t, a checkerboard contrasts within-side similarities with cross-boundary similarities in a context of ±k frames. Values are normalized for display; local maxima exceeding the selected relative threshold are highlighted. This is a basic Foote-style novelty detector, not a model of harmonic analysis or formal functions.

Sampling at fixed seconds (rather than musical beats) means expressive tempo changes can distort diagonal repetitions. Audio–beat alignment and tempo-invariant representations will be discussed in later modules.
